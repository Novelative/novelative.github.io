How to Spot and Fix Plot Holes Before You Start Your Second Draft

You’ve finally finished your first draft! Completing a manuscript is a massive accomplishment that deserves celebration. But as any experienced author will tell you, the first draft is just the beginning. Now comes the hard work: evaluating your story and plugging the plot holes. 

A plot hole is a glaring inconsistency, omission, or error in your storyline. It happens when your narrative violates its own established rules, which shatters the reader's immersion and destroys their trust in your story world. Before you dive into writing your second draft, you need to identify these logic gaps and fix them. Here is how to spot and mend plot holes so your readers stay completely engrossed in your world.

## Step 1: Learn to Spot the "Big 7" Plot Holes

Plot holes usually aren't about real-world logic; they happen because an author forgets a constraint they established earlier in the book. Look out for these common culprits:
* **Capability Holes:** A character suddenly does something you previously established they couldn't do (like swimming across a river when chapter two said they couldn't swim).
* **Geography and Travel Holes:** A journey that took three days early in the book miraculously takes half a day later on.
* **Knowledge Holes:** A character acts on information they have no logical way of knowing.
* **Motive Holes:** A character acts completely against their established motivations without any narrative explanation for the shift.
* **Timeline Holes:** Events happen in a contradictory sequence or seasons don't align logically.
* **Object Holes:** An item appears, disappears, or changes out of nowhere (like a sword changing from black to silver, or an empty gun suddenly firing). 
* **Consequence Holes:** A major action occurs that *should* have huge consequences in your world, but the story moves on as if nothing happened.

## Step 2: Use These Techniques to Find the Gaps

You can't fix what you can't see. Because plot holes are often separated by dozens of chapters and tens of thousands of words, they can be incredibly difficult to spot. Try these diagnostic methods:

**The Shelf Method**
Don't look at your first draft right after finishing it. Put your manuscript on the "shelf" for at least six weeks. Coming back to it with a fresh perspective is crucial for seeing the narrative as a reader would, rather than what you *intended* to write. 

**Do a Chronological Readthrough**
Read your entire story in sequence without making edits. Resist the urge to tweak sentences. This allows you to experience the overarching flow and makes inconsistencies more obvious. 

**Build a Plot Grid (Reverse Outlining)**
Create a spreadsheet to retroactively outline what you actually wrote. Use columns to track chapter numbers, timelines, your main plot, and individual subplots. This bird's-eye view makes it painfully obvious if a subplot abruptly disappears or if your pacing is off.

**The 3-Step Canon Audit**
If you want to be meticulous, create a two-column document. In the left column, list every established rule, capability, and travel time in your world. In the right column, list the scenes that rely on those facts. If a scene contradicts your left column, flag it. Don't try to fix it yet—just gather all the inconsistencies first.

## Step 3: Fix the Plot Holes

Once you've flagged the problems, it’s time to solve them. Here are a few reliable ways to patch your story:

* **Binary Resolution:** When a scene contradicts a rule, you have two choices: rewrite the scene so it follows the rule, or rewrite the rule itself. You can never leave both in contradiction.
* **Subtraction is Addition:** If a subplot or scene creates more logical problems than it's worth, be prepared to "kill your darlings" and cut it entirely.
* **Insert Foreshadowing:** If an event feels like it comes out of nowhere, backtrack into earlier chapters and drop subtle hints to make the moment feel planned.
* **Enhance Motivations:** If a character acts out of character to serve the plot, you need to go back and build the emotional groundwork that justifies their new choices.
